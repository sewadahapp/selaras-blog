The first sound in *After Rain* was not made in a studio. It was the low,
uneven hum of a train leaving Bandung just before sunrise.

![A commuter train waiting under the lights of Gedebage Station at night. Photograph by NFarras, CC BY-SA 4.0.](/images/commuter-line-bandung-raya-in-gedebage-station.jpg)

## Start with attention

Field recording is less about collecting impressive sounds and more about
learning to notice what is already there. I keep a small recorder in my bag,
but the important part is the habit of listening:

1. Find a place with a distinct rhythm.
2. Record longer than feels necessary.
3. Write down where and when the sound was captured.
4. Return to it later, without trying to force it into a song.

## Let it change on the way

The distance between the original place and the studio is where the music
starts to change. Most of the recording ends up passing through a few pieces
of outboard gear before it sits inside a song — a little compression, a long
delay, nothing that hides where it came from.

![An effects rack with a Yamaha SPX90 and a Peavey DPM Spectrum in a home studio. Photograph by Klara Kopf, CC BY 2.0.](/images/effects-rack-yamaha-spx90-peavey-dpm-spectrum.jpg)
